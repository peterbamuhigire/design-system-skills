# Colour-Choice Procedure

Parent skill: [`../SKILL.md`](../SKILL.md) (`color-selection`).

**When to read:** at the start of any palette generation for a brand, campaign, website or
document, and when a client rejects colours. Sources (human design authority): Adams, Dawson,
Foster and Seddon (2012, revised edition) *Graphic Design Rules*, Frances Lincoln (Colour
section); the Kodak/Topping colour-choice guidance as reported by Kupsh and Graves (1993) *How to
Create High-Impact Business Presentations*, NTC Business Books (background first, lightness
before hue). Colour-psychology claims in older books are weak evidence; use them only as hints.

---

## 1. Client colour brief (ask early; ask again after feedback)

| Question | Why |
|---|---|
| Existing standards or colours that must stay? | Existing equity is an asset |
| Colours to avoid, and why? | Personal, organisational, rival-school, political or religious history |
| Which markets and cultures will see this? | Meanings differ by market |
| Who is the audience, including age? | Children respond to fewer, brighter colours; older audiences accept broader palettes |
| How long must the brand live? | Long-life brands avoid trend colours |
| Print, screen or both? | Gamut, spot-ink cost, dark mode |
| Budget for spot inks or special finishes? | Constrains print builds |

When a client rejects a colour, ask: "Is it this exact shade, or the whole family?"

## 2. Competitor colour audit

1. List the category leaders and direct competitors in the client's market (at least five).
2. Record each one's primary and accent colours (sample from logos, signage, websites, uniforms).
3. Plot them on a simple hue and lightness map.
4. Note category conventions (for example finance clustering on navy and green; telecoms owning
   bright yellow or red in East Africa - verify current brand colours before citing them).
5. Decide: **conform** (signals category membership) or **stand apart** (signals difference).
   Record the decision and reason. Conformity by accident is the failure.
6. During election periods, add national and party colours to the map.

## 3. Order of decisions

1. **Temperature:** warm or cool family, or one deliberate injection of the opposite.
2. **Background first:** the largest area sets the mood; choose it before objects on it.
3. **Lightness before hue:** mood comes more from lightness and saturation than from hue.
4. **Hue:** sourced from the brand anchor, imagery, a real place (sample a photograph: a
   papyrus swamp is not "green" but olive, straw, silver and brown) or a painter's palette.
5. **Accent:** one vivid colour reserved for action or emphasis; vivid colours are accents,
   large areas look bolder so tone them down.
6. **When in doubt:** achromatic plus one colour, or black plus two colours.

## 4. Tonal and adjacency checks

- **Greyscale test:** convert to greyscale; hues of equal tone merge into one shape. Separate them
  by lightness.
- **Analogous neighbours** placed side by side can turn muddy; separate or change tone.
- **High-intensity complements** vibrate; use only when vibration is the idea.
- **Value contrast** directs attention; pure black and white stands out among mid-tone clutter.
- **Anti-beige:** timid desaturated palettes read as error; one confident block of an unexpected
  pairing reads as intentional.
- **Calm or tension:** lower vibrancy and pale tints calm (health, safety, dentistry); warm
  advancing on cool, or an unexpected object colour, creates tension.
- **Light and surroundings:** complex colours (violets, greys) shift more under different light;
  warn the client and view proofs under agreed light.
- **Colour-vision deficiency:** never rely on colour alone; see `accessible-color-and-contrast`.

## 5. Culture and meaning

- Research meanings in each market with local respondents; do not rely on lists of "colour
  meanings". Mourning colours, religious associations and party colours vary and change.
- Colour plus time equals brand: consistent ownership of one colour over years becomes the asset.
  Do not switch for novelty; a challenger should not fight the leader on the leader's colour.

## 6. Colour rationale page (for the brand guide or proposal)

Slot template per colour:
"Our [colour name] was chosen to [stand apart from / align with] a category dominated by
[competitor colours]. It is [warm/cool] to signal [attribute], and it was checked with
[respondents] in [market], where [cultural note]."

Attach: the competitor map, the temperature and lightness decision, the source image or artwork,
the cultural check log and the accessibility hand-off to `color-system-and-palette`.

## 7. Presentation colour notes

- Dark backgrounds with a light, dominant title colour and a secondary colour for bullets create
  hierarchy; light-to-dark or grey-to-bright progressions show order; a rainbow implies equal
  importance.
- Test projected colours in the room; projectors wash out light tints.
