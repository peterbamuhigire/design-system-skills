# Copy and Image Constructions

Parent skill: [`../SKILL.md`](../SKILL.md) (`advertising-creative-art-direction`).

**When to read:** when forming a concept's key visual and headline relationship, and when laying
out any advertisement, social unit, poster or point-of-sale piece.

Sources: Landa (2022) *Strategic Creativity*, Routledge (constructions, straight/crooked rule,
non-redundancy); Serling (ed.) (2002) *How to Write Million Dollar Ads, Sales Letters and Web
Marketing Pieces* (poster test, product as hero, captions, layout practice from direct-response
practitioners); Adams et al. (2012) *Graphic Design Rules* (images support the text without
repeating it; the unexpected pairing is remembered). Claimed test results in those sources are
historical anecdotes, not evidence; test in the client's own market.

---

## 1. The principle: one plus one makes three

Headline and image together must communicate more than either alone. Never repeat in words what
the picture already shows. Let the viewer complete the thought.

**Cover tests (run on every concept):**
1. Cover the headline and read the image. What does it say alone?
2. Cover the image and read the headline. What does it say alone?
3. Neither element alone should carry the whole message, and neither should be meaningless.
   If the image alone says everything, cut words. If the headline alone says everything, the image
   is decoration; replace it with one that adds meaning.

**Poster test:** headline plus visual must sell the idea with no body copy. If the concept needs
the body copy to make sense, it is not yet a concept.

## 2. Three constructions

| Construction | How it works | Best when | Watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copy-led | The headline or typography is the star; imagery is minimal or absent | Professional audiences reading closely; category where the argument is the difference; outdoor type-only statements | The headline must beat people's preference for pictures |
| Image-led | The image carries the message; words give context | Multilingual or lower-literacy audiences; fast-scrolling feeds; outdoor at speed | The image must be unambiguous without its caption |
| Fused (emblematic) | Words and image are inseparable (type built into the picture, or the picture forming a word) | Brand campaigns seeking memorability | Legibility at small sizes; translation cost |

**East African note:** in markets that mix English, Kiswahili, Luganda, Runyankore and other
languages, image-led and fused constructions travel across language groups. Copy-led works for
urban professionals on LinkedIn and in business print. For radio-heavy plans, the "image" is the
sound design; keep the visual units consistent with it.

## 3. Straight and crooked

- If the image is odd, funny or shocking, keep the headline straight.
- If the headline is witty or provocative, keep the image straight.
- If both are straight, the ad is ignored. If both are clever, they compete.
- Ironic contrast (a pleasant image with a sharp line) is a deliberate crooked move; decide
  which element does the heavy lifting.

## 4. Ad layout rules

1. **Product, user or proof as hero.** A large, simple product or result photograph on a quiet
   ground usually beats a borrowed-interest scene that needs a strained bridge headline.
2. **Relatable visuals.** Show people and situations the audience recognises as theirs; shoot
   locally where the audience would notice imported settings.
3. **Captions are read.** The eye tends to go image then caption; make the caption restate a
   benefit or add a fact, never describe the obvious.
4. **Face and quote.** A real customer's face with a short quotation beside it is a reusable,
   credible layout (with consent and a real quotation).
5. **Headlines in upper and lower case.** Reserve reversed type for short headlines, not body
   copy.
6. **Drop caps** can cue where to start reading long copy.
7. **Editorial-style layouts** must be clearly labelled as advertising where the host medium or
   regulator requires it. Never disguise an ad as editorial content.
8. **One focal point, one takeaway,** one call to action.
9. **Type is chosen by the engine,** not by the source books' era-specific examples; route faces
   through `font-selection-and-pairing`.

## 5. Thumbnail and scroll tests

- Covers, packs, product images and social units must read as tiny thumbnails; most people meet
  them that size first.
- View every unit at actual phone size, in the feed or placement mock, with interface overlays in
  place. If the offer or brand is not identifiable in the first glance, rework.
- Check that text-free zones required by the placement are respected (see
  `campaign-systems-and-channel-adaptation.md` section 5).

## 6. Image choice and ethics in advertising

- Every image carries political content: audiences read age, gender, ethnicity, class and power
  from any group shot. Decide deliberately.
- Crop consciously: face-dominant framing reads as intellect and authority, body-dominant as
  physicality; apply framing consistently across genders.
- Show people as agents with dignity, especially in development, health and NGO advertising.
  No "poverty" imagery; follow consent and safeguarding policy for beneficiaries and children.
- Shock imagery only when the brief calls for it and the client signs off likely reactions.
- Do not disguise weak images with filters; get a better image or make the treatment a deliberate
  idea. See `photography-art-direction` for sourcing and rights.
