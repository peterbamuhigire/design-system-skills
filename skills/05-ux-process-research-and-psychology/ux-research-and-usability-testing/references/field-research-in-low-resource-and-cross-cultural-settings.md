# Field Research in Low-Resource and Cross-Cultural Settings

Parent skill: [`../SKILL.md`](../SKILL.md) (`ux-research-and-usability-testing`).

**When to read:** when planning research with people whose literacy, language, devices,
connectivity, social norms or institutions differ from the research team's; when research
happens in villages, markets, clinics, schools, agent shops or savings-group meetings rather than
a lab; when a government body, NGO or cooperative stands between the team and the users; and when
the product will affect families or communities, not only the person holding the phone.

Method choice stays with `research-method-selector.md` and session mechanics with
`usability-test-protocol.md`. This file adapts both to the field.

---

## 1. Before fieldwork: access, permission and positionality

| Step | Do | Failure if skipped |
|---|---|---|
| Map the gatekeepers | List every authority whose permission is expected (district officials, local council chair, head teacher, facility in-charge, cooperative chair, religious or clan leaders) | Sessions cancelled on arrival; participants afraid to speak |
| Obtain permissions in the order the hierarchy expects | Start at the top of the relevant hierarchy; carry letters of introduction | Lower-level gatekeepers refuse without sign-off from above |
| Prepare an introduction card | One page with photos of the team, the sponsor, the purpose in the local language and a local contact number | Strangers with notebooks are mistaken for tax, land or political agents |
| State positionality | Write down who the team is, who pays, what the team gains, and how that could bias what people say | Participants tell the sponsor what they think it wants |
| Check research ethics and data law | Follow the client's ethics process; for Uganda, design consent and data handling to the Data Protection and Privacy Act, 2019 and confirm current requirements with counsel | Unlawful processing of health, financial or children's data |
| Plan compensation | Agree fair, non-coercive compensation (airtime, data, transport refund, a meal) with local advisers | Over-payment distorts answers; under-payment exploits time |

## 2. Adapt the instruments to the participants

- **Assume nothing is read.** Read questions aloud; record answers by writing, drawing or voice.
  A printed workbook becomes an interviewer's prompt, not a participant's form.
- **Use pictures, objects and maps.** Photo cards, drawn scenarios, real receipts, the actual
  phone, a hand-drawn map of "where you go for help" produce more than abstract questions.
- **Start with daily life.** Mapping a typical day, a household's money flows or a patient's
  journey to care surfaces the context before the product is mentioned.
- **Talk about people.** Participants often speak more readily about family, neighbours and
  helpers than about themselves; use that to understand shared decisions.
- **Translate for meaning, then back-translate.** Pilot each instrument with two or three
  participants and revise before the main round.
- **Use local interviewers and interpreters,** trained in neutral probing and briefed on the
  study's purpose; debrief after each session to catch what the translation flattened.
- **Match interviewer and participant where norms require it** (for example women interviewing
  women on health topics).

## 3. Session formats that work in the field

| Format | Use when | Adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| Contextual observation at the point of use | The task happens in a place (agent counter, clinic queue, market stall) | Observe first, ask later; note interruptions, helpers and workarounds |
| Individual interview in a private setting | Sensitive topics (money, health, family conflict) | Choose the place with the participant; allow a trusted companion if asked |
| Facilitated group session | Community decisions, co-design, conflicting stakeholder views | Use the facilitation rules in section 4 |
| Messaging-app diary study | Behaviour over weeks; participants already use a messaging app daily | Short prompts, voice notes allowed, clear data-handling notice, never ask for sensitive data in a group chat |
| Low-fidelity prototype test on the participant's own phone | Evaluating flows | Paper or clickable prototype; test offline behaviour and data cost explicitly |
| Intermediary shadowing | Agents, health workers or teachers mediate the service | Observe both the intermediary and the end user; they have different goals |

## 4. Facilitating group sessions without letting power decide

Hierarchy in the room (a manager, an official, an elder, a male head of household) silences
others. Structure the session so every contribution is heard and recorded:

1. **Agree the ground rules aloud**: one voice at a time; the facilitator decides who speaks next;
   concerns are welcome and will be recorded, not argued.
2. **Keep four visible lists** (flip chart or wall): *Solutions* (ideas for what to do),
   *Concerns* (why an idea may fail), *Information* (facts, history, constraints, resources) and
   *Questions we are trying to answer* (each written as "How might we ...?").
3. **Reflect back before recording.** Repeat what the person said in your words, ask "Did I get
   it right?", then write it. The person, not the facilitator, confirms the wording.
4. **Route concerns through the facilitator,** not across the room. Finish hearing the current
   speaker, then turn to the person with the concern, record it, and ask what they would do
   instead.
5. **Manage dominant voices kindly.** Summarise what the dominant speaker has contributed, point
   to where it is recorded, and invite others; promise to return to them.
6. **Ask the sponsor to state their fallback plan at the start** ("if we cannot agree, this is
   probably what will happen") so the group improves on something concrete and the sponsor's
   authority is visible rather than hidden.
7. **Watch the question change.** When the real problem shifts, add a new "How might we" rather
   than forcing the discussion back.
8. **Close with a shared summary** read back to the group, and agree what happens next and when
   the group will hear back.

Do not use this format to choose between a few pre-decided options, or where participants cannot
safely speak openly; use individual interviews instead.

## 5. Analysis lens: needs, values, capabilities and impact

Code findings at three levels (individual, family or household, community) against three
questions: what the person *needs*, what they *value*, and what they are *able* to do given their
circumstances. Then test each concept against four impact questions before recommending it:

| Question | Ask |
|---|---|
| Preferred future | What behaviour or situation should this make more likely, and is that what the community wants? |
| Impact | Who else is affected (agents, family members, competitors, public services) and how? |
| System | What infrastructure, policy or institution must also change for this to work? |
| Alignment | Does it align users, the business, the community and the environment, or trade one off silently? |

Record contradictions between what people say and what the team expected; do not smooth them
away. Contradictions are often the most valuable finding.

## 6. Participation, ownership and sustainability

- Involve the people affected in defining the problem, not only in testing the answer.
- Identify who will maintain content, train new users and fix problems after the project ends;
  if nobody local can, the design is not finished.
- Return findings to participants in a form they can use (a spoken summary, pictures, a short
  printed sheet in their language).
- Agree who owns and can access the data and content collected; for community knowledge
  (songs, stories, records), the community's control is the default.
- Watch for research fatigue in communities studied repeatedly; coordinate with other teams and
  share data where consent allows rather than re-interviewing.

## 7. Evidence standard

- Report sample, place, dates, language, interpreter use and gatekeepers, so readers can judge
  what the findings represent.
- Separate observed behaviour, participant statements and team interpretation in the synthesis.
- Qualitative field findings are directional; do not convert them into population percentages.
- Mark any product decision not traced to field evidence as an assumption.

## 8. Worked example (original): research plan for a farm-input credit product

**Question.** How do smallholder maize farmers in two districts of eastern Uganda decide to buy
seed and fertiliser on credit, and who is involved?

| Plan element | Decision |
|---|---|
| Gatekeepers | District agricultural officer, sub-county chief, farmer cooperative chairs; introduction letters from the lending partner |
| Team | Two local researchers fluent in Lusoga and Ateso, one designer; women interview women |
| Instruments | Picture cards of inputs and prices; a household money-flow map drawn with the family; the participant's own phone for a paper prototype of the repayment flow |
| Sessions | 16 household interviews, 4 agro-dealer shadowing days, 2 facilitated cooperative sessions using the four lists |
| Consent and data | Verbal consent read in the local language and recorded; no national ID numbers collected; photos only with explicit consent |
| Compensation | Airtime equivalent agreed with the cooperative, plus lunch at group sessions |
| Return of findings | Spoken summary and picture sheet presented at the cooperative's next meeting |

Expected output: decision journeys at household level, the agro-dealer's role as trusted
adviser, repayment timing against harvest cycles, and a list of assumptions to test in the
prototype round.

## 9. Premium versus generic output

| Generic | Senior |
|---|---|
| Sends an online survey in English | Designs instruments for how participants actually communicate |
| Interviews "users" in isolation | Researches households, intermediaries and institutions around the user |
| Treats participants as data sources | Returns findings and plans local ownership |
| Reports "82% want the feature" from 11 interviews | Reports directional findings with sample and context |
| Ignores the team's own position | States who funded, who asked, and how that may bias answers |
