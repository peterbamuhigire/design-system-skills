# Live Presentation Readiness: Brief, Build, Room and Review

Parent skill: [`../SKILL.md`](../SKILL.md) (`deck-system`).

**When to read:** before building any deck that will be presented live (pitch, board paper,
workshop, training, tender oral), when the venue is unseen, when handouts are needed, and when
rehearsing. This engine owns the visual and room-legibility side. Pitch content and rehearsal
coaching belong to `business-plan-skills`; tender orals to `proposal-skills`; event logistics to
the marketing engine's events playbooks.

---

## 1. Inputs: the presentation brief

Collect these before the first slide. Missing items are risks to name, not to guess.

| Input | What to capture | Why it changes the deck |
|---|---|---|
| Decision sought | What the audience must believe, decide or do by the end | Sets the closing slide and every action title |
| Audience | Roles, who decides, prior knowledge, acronyms they know, formality, what would offend | Sets depth, vocabulary and tone |
| Slot | Length, position in the programme, speakers before and after, event theme | Sets pace and what can be cut live |
| Room | Size, seat layout (fixed or movable), stage or floor level | Sets type size, layout and interaction |
| Display | Projector, LED wall or television; aspect ratio; inputs; clicker; microphone; audio for video | Sets slide format and media risk |
| Power and network | Generator or UPS, internet reliability | Decides offline copies and fallbacks |
| Materials | Handouts, props, flip chart, printing access | Decides leave-behind design and timing |
| Protocol | Order of precedence, opening prayer or formalities, language mix, late-start habits | Protects the opening and a hard close |
| Contacts | Venue technician, organiser, the person introducing you | Lets you test equipment and send your introduction |

## 2. Decision rules

| Condition | Decision | Wrong-choice failure |
|---|---|---|
| Deck will be presented live | Sparse slides, one idea each, detail in notes | Slides become a teleprompter the room reads instead of listening |
| Deck will circulate without a presenter | Fuller captions and a leave-behind version | Readers lack the spoken argument |
| Room is large or the back row is far | Enlarge or cut text until it passes the farthest-seat test | Text is unreadable beyond the front rows |
| Display ratio unknown | Confirm before building; bring both 16:9 and 4:3 exports if still unknown | Letterboxing shrinks every element |
| Power or projection unreliable | PDF and native file offline, printed handouts, flip-chart fallback | The talk stops when equipment fails |
| Several charts compare the same measure | One locked axis scale across the series | Unequal values look equal |
| Audience includes senior officials short of time | One-page summary handout alongside the deck | The decision-maker never reads the deck |
| Group work planned but seats are fixed rows | Switch to pair discussion with neighbours | Planned activity collapses on arrival |

## 3. Procedure

**Build**
1. Outline the argument in order, then storyboard one card per point and choose its form:
   statement, number, chart, diagram or photograph. Re-sequence cards, not slides.
2. Write the opening and the close last, once the body is ordered.
3. Plan more material than the slot needs and mark what you will cut live; never speed up to fit.
4. Apply the slide rules: action titles; parallel list items; acronyms spelled out on first use;
   sentence case with emphasis by size and weight; one dominant element per slide; two or three
   type, colour and image-style choices fixed on the master; flush-left text; landscape format.
5. Put a title slide up as people arrive. Use builds only to control sequence.

**Room check (arrive 45-60 minutes early)**
1. Sit in the farthest seat with the real deck on the real display. Anything unreadable is cut or
   enlarged; this test outranks any fixed point size.
2. Check aspect ratio, focus, microphone, audio, clicker range and lighting (dim near the screen,
   enough light in the seats for notes and to see the presenter). The bottom of the image must sit
   high enough that heads in front do not block it.
3. Tape cables across walkways; know the exits and the room's safe capacity.
4. Place handouts according to the timing plan (section 5).

**Deliver**
- Stand beside the screen, not in the beam; blank the screen (black slide or the B key) when
  talking off-slide; say more than the slide says; repeat audience questions before answering.

**Review (same day)**
- Record three things that worked, three to improve, questions owed with owners and dates,
  handout uptake, and revisions for next time.

## 4. Seating layouts

| Layout | Choose when | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Theatre rows | Launches and lectures for large groups | Little interaction |
| Classroom (rows with tables) | Training with note-taking | Fewer seats than theatre |
| Curved or chevron rows | Training where people should see each other | More floor space |
| U-shape | Discussion; presenter moves inside the U | Fewer seats; screen at the open end |
| Rounds or clusters | Workshops with group tasks | Weaker sightlines to the screen |
| Boardroom | Small decision meetings | Screen visible from one end only |

Screen-size and viewing-distance rules of thumb in older literature predate current displays;
check the room against the venue's AV guidance or a current image-size standard and prove it
with the farthest-seat test.

## 5. Handouts and leave-behinds

| Situation | Timing | Design |
|---|---|---|
| Audience must refer to figures during the talk | Before, with a clear "look now, listen now" cue | Selected key slides with space for notes |
| Material would distract | After | Summary plus a QR code or link to the PDF |
| Reference material for some attendees | Table at the exit | Collated pack, colour-coded sections if long |
| Senior officials or board members | With the invitation or at the start | One-page summary of decision, evidence and ask |

Leave about half of each page empty; headings work as signposts; send a shareable PDF (email or
WhatsApp) afterwards. Handouts left on seats are feedback on their usefulness.

## 6. Rehearsal scoring (internal)

Two colleagues score each rehearsal of a pitch, oral or workshop:

| Area | Points | Judged on |
|---|---|---|
| Content | 10 | Accurate; right depth for this audience; claims supported |
| Structure | 10 | Purpose stated early; visible structure; clean transitions; close tied to the body |
| Visuals and media | 20 | Legible from the back; each visual adds evidence; equipment handled smoothly; handouts useful |
| Delivery | 10 | In command; natural movement; voice and pace; eye contact; dress suited to the audience |

Reweight for the purpose and record the weights used.

## 7. Worked example (original)

A district health team in Mbarara presents a maternal-health budget to district councillors in a
council hall with fixed benches, an ageing 4:3 projector and frequent power cuts. The brief
records councillors as the decision-makers, English and Runyankore in use, and a prayer and
protocol opening. Decisions: build in 4:3; limit each slide to one district comparison on a
locked axis; print a one-page Runyankore-English summary with the budget ask; carry the deck as
PDF on a laptop with a charged power bank; pair discussion replaces the planned group exercise.
The farthest-bench test removes two tables that become handout pages.

## 8. Claims not to import

Do not repeat figures about how much people learn through their eyes, fixed retention gains from
visuals, colour raising sales by set percentages, "left-brain/right-brain" learning styles, or
colours calming people. They are poorly sourced or discredited.

## 9. Presenter kit

Laptop and charger; deck as native file and PDF offline and on a USB drive; display adapters;
clicker and spare batteries; extension lead and tape; printed notes; backup handouts; markers for a
flip-chart fallback; power bank or small UPS; a phone that can share its connection.

Sources: practice synthesised from Kupsh and Graves, *How to Create High-Impact Business
Presentations* (room, handout and rehearsal checklists); Duarte, *HBR Guide to Persuasive
Presentations* (one idea per slide, action titles); Reynolds, *Presentation Zen* (restraint).
